# Music Public Performance Licensing for Youth/Amateur Sports Walk-Up Songs

*General public information only — not legal advice. Compiled from public web sources on 2026-10-08 for scoping purposes.*

## What do ASCAP/BMI/SESAC/GMR license, and who needs a public performance license?

### Takeaway
US copyright law requires anyone who plays copyrighted recorded or live music outside a private home/family circle — including nearly any business or publicly-accessible venue — to hold a public performance license from the relevant performing rights organization(s) (PRO); ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR each represent a different (sometimes overlapping, sometimes exclusive) catalog of songwriters/publishers, so one license does not cover another PRO's catalog.

### Cited Findings
- "All businesses, such as radio stations, restaurants, retail stores, spas and concert venues, that publicly perform music are required by law to obtain a Public Performance License from ASCAP, BMI and SESAC in the US." — [ASCAP & BMI Music Licensing Guide for Businesses](https://soundsuit.fm/bmi-and-ascap-music-licensing-for-businesses/)
- A "public performance" is defined to include any music played outside a normal circle of friends and family in a public place, or transmitted to the public via radio, TV, digital services, etc. — [BMI Music Licensing FAQs](https://www.bmi.com/licensing/faqs)
- "Technically, anyone performing music publicly anywhere has to pay — for example, if you are in a marching band playing in a parade and the song is an ASCAP or BMI song, the band or the parade organizers have to pay." — summarized from general PRO background search results, no single primary source isolated (see Gaps)
- If you already hold a license from one PRO (e.g., ASCAP), it does not cover another PRO's catalog (e.g., BMI) — licensees may need multiple PRO licenses to be fully covered. — [BMI Music Licensing FAQs](https://www.bmi.com/licensing/faqs)
- SESAC is a for-profit, invitation-only-membership PRO, smaller than ASCAP/BMI; Global Music Rights (GMR), founded 2013 by Irving Azoff, is also for-profit, invitation-only, and has the smallest catalog of the four major PROs, with a "boutique" service model. — [ASCAP vs BMI vs SESAC vs GMR: PROs Explained](https://radiocreator.com/ascap-bmi-sesac-gmr-explained/)
- Licensees typically obtain a "blanket license" — a flat fee or revenue-percentage license covering any song in that PRO's full catalog for a year, rather than licensing individual tracks. — [Who are ASCAP and BMI? FAQ](https://www.acbands.org/assets/docs/webdoc/ACB-BlanketPerformanceLicense-FAQ-2012-01.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because PRO catalogs don't overlap fully, a venue/organization wanting broad legal coverage for "whatever song a kid picks" would in practice need ASCAP + BMI coverage at minimum (SESAC/GMR catalogs are far smaller and less likely to contain a given pop walk-up song, but not guaranteed irrelevant).
- The "private, non-commercial, family/friends" carve-out is the only clearly non-licensed scenario — a youth sports field open to spectators/the public does not obviously fall inside that carve-out once a loudspeaker projects music to everyone in attendance, including outside the player's "normal circle of family or friends."

### Gaps
- I could not isolate a single ASCAP/BMI/SESAC primary-source page with an explicit, citable "who is exempt" list (e.g., the Fairness in Music Licensing Act's small-business exemptions for TV/radio retransmission) specific to recorded-music-via-speaker scenarios; most retrieved pages were secondary/aggregator sites (SoundMachine, CloudCoverMusic, SoundSuit, etc.) rather than ASCAP.com/BMI.com/SESAC.com/GMR pages themselves, so some of the above should be treated as consensus-of-secondary-sources rather than confirmed primary-source quotes.

## Do youth sports facilities / Little League complexes / high school athletic departments / travel-ball venues typically hold a blanket PRO license?

### Takeaway
High school athletics have a clear, documented answer via the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS): NFHS has secured a blanket ASCAP license and BMI separately offers a "License for Primary and Secondary Schools" that explicitly covers sporting events, walk-up/warm-up music, and livestreaming — schools are expected to hold one of these. For Little League complexes and travel-ball tournament venues specifically, I found no public information either way (a real gap), and multiple secondary sources (including a walk-up-song app's own licensing page) state that it varies by facility and the team/parent is advised to check with the facility operator rather than assume coverage.

### Cited Findings
- "Schools need performance licenses to play recorded music at athletic events... using 'recorded music (perhaps the latest new pop song) as warm-up music' requires licensing because it's not their own students performing it." — [NFHS: Copyright Compliance with Music and Sporting Events](https://nfhs.org/stories/copyright-compliance-with-music-and-sporting-events)
- NFHS has secured a blanket license from ASCAP covering recorded music use and livestreaming of events; BMI separately offers a "License for Primary and Secondary Schools" covering playback and broadcast. — [NFHS: Copyright Compliance with Music and Sporting Events](https://nfhs.org/stories/copyright-compliance-with-music-and-sporting-events)
- The BMI license for K-12 schools explicitly lists "sporting events," "games," and "pep rallies" as covered uses, alongside concerts, musicals, dances, and fundraisers; it covers multiple schools/districts under one license including off-site events; it does not cover "grand rights" for full Broadway-style shows. — [BMI License (NFHS copyright portal)](https://copyright.nfhs.org/bmi-license/)
- "The responsibility [for public performance licensing of walk-up songs] generally belongs with the park, league, school, municipality or facility — not the individual parent, coach or Team DJ pressing play. Many parks, leagues, schools, municipalities and other venues already maintain blanket music licensing that covers commercial music played at their facilities and sporting events." — [BallparkDJ: Music Licensing for Walk-Up Songs & Youth Sports](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)
- BallparkDJ advises teams to "check with their facility operator" to determine whether coverage already exists, rather than assuming it — [BallparkDJ: Music Licensing](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)
- A separate specialized vendor, Neptune GameTime, markets itself as providing "public performance licensing that allows schools to legally play music during sporting events" plus a 50,000+ song database — implying this is sold as a standalone product precisely because many schools/venues do not already have blanket coverage sorted out on their own. — [search result summary citing Neptune GameTime](https://nfhs.org/stories/copyright-compliance-with-music-and-sporting-events) (secondary reference within NFHS search results)

### Inferences
- High school athletics is the one tier of "youth sports" with a documented, named, institution-level licensing answer (NFHS/ASCAP + BMI school license). Travel-ball, Little League, and most town/municipal rec leagues have no equivalent documented program that surfaced in search — the honest reading is that coverage is inconsistent and ad hoc, dependent on whether the specific town/county parks department or private complex operator separately holds a PRO license (some municipalities do hold ASCAP/BMI "municipality" licenses for unrelated reasons, e.g. parks/civic events — see the ASCAP municipal license agreement found below — but there is no evidence this is universal or typically extended to cover a specific baseball diamond's walk-up music).
- A software product in this space (BallparkDJ) treats "does the venue already have a license" as something the *end user* must verify, not something the app itself resolves — consistent with the broader pattern of apps pushing the licensing question downstream (see next section).

### Gaps
- No direct public statement from Little League International, USSSA, Perfect Game, or other major travel-ball sanctioning bodies about music licensing was found. This is plausibly because it's simply not addressed by those organizations publicly, or because it wasn't surfaced by search — treat as an open question, not a confirmed absence.
- No confirmation of what fraction of municipal parks-and-rec departments or private travel-ball complexes actually hold ASCAP/BMI licenses in practice (only that "many" do, per one vendor's marketing page, which is a self-interested secondary source).

## Is there a distinct "sports venue"/"stadium" license category, and do smaller/youth venues get one vs. larger pro/college venues?

### Takeaway
Yes — ASCAP has a named "Sports and Competitive Events" license (distinct from its general business/retail licenses) and BMI has a "Professional League Music License"; these are explicitly scaled toward leagues/teams/venues of real size (the ASCAP license requires reporting of all preseason/regular-season/playoff events), and the clear, confirmed pattern is that large pro/college venues and "universities" maintain these institutional blanket licenses — there's no evidence of an equivalent, named "youth/amateur small-venue" tier being commonly adopted, aside from the K-12-school-specific BMI/ASCAP licenses described above.

### Cited Findings
- "With ASCAP, you will need a Sports and Competitive Events license to use music within their library... Professional sports applications will need a Professional League Music License from BMI." — [search result citing cloudcovermusic.com: Playing Music at Sporting Events](https://cloudcovermusic.com/music-licensing-guide/play-music-at-sporting-events)
- ASCAP's Sports & Competitive Event license names the responsible party as "the sports leagues, teams, producers, promoters, or hosts of the competitive event," who are also responsible for "reporting all team/competitive events, including all preseason, regular season, playoff, finals and championship events." — [ASCAP Sports & Competitive Event License](https://www.ascap.com/music-users/types/sports-and-competitive-events)
- Examples of events the ASCAP Sports and Competitive Events license is marketed to cover include "Major, Minor, Independent, Amateur Leagues, Arenas and Auditoriums, Baseball, Basketball, Boxing, College Conferences, Endurance Races, Football, Hockey, and Lacrosse" — notably this list explicitly includes "Amateur Leagues," suggesting ASCAP's own marketing contemplates amateur-level licensees, not just pro/college. — [ASCAP Sports & Competitive Event License](https://www.ascap.com/music-users/types/sports-and-competitive-events)
- "Many universities already maintain institutional blanket licenses with these organizations, which allow music to be performed inside the stadium." — [search summary citing sportcenter.us: Music Laws & Sports Entertainment](https://sportcenter.us/the-legislative-game-plan-how-music-laws-affect-sports-enter)
- "It is the responsibility of the host of the venue to have a public performance license." — [search summary, general sports-venue licensing sources](https://www.starvartists.com/blog/music-licensing-sporting-events)

### Inferences
- ASCAP's own marketing copy using the phrase "Amateur Leagues" is notable evidence that the PRO's licensing category is *designed* to reach down to amateur levels, not just pro/college — but confirmed, documented uptake by amateur leagues/youth facilities specifically (vs. the license merely being nominally available to them) was not found.
- The gap between "a license category exists and is marketed broadly" and "youth venues actually buy it" is the crux of the real-world risk surface: the license existing doesn't mean a given Little League field or travel-ball complex has purchased it.

### Gaps
- No primary ASCAP/BMI pricing or market-penetration data for amateur/youth-level licensees was found — can't say what fraction of youth venues hold this license vs. the K-12 school-specific license vs. nothing at all.

## How do apps in adjacent spaces (karaoke, fitness, DJ/wedding, walk-up-song) describe their legal responsibility in ToS/marketing?

### Takeaway
The consistent pattern across every adjacent-space app examined is the same: the app frames itself as providing *access* or *technical convenience*, explicitly disclaims providing any public-performance rights, and places responsibility for obtaining the necessary PRO licensing onto the venue/organization/end user — this is stated directly and prominently in at least one walk-up-song-specific app's marketing (BallparkDJ) and in karaoke-app terms of service (My Karaoke Party).

### Cited Findings
- My Karaoke Party's terms restrict the app to "personal use only... private enjoyment, in a non-commercial setting," and state users "may not use the app in any public place, such as bars, restaurants, clubs, or any other venues where the public has access." The terms place responsibility on users "for ensuring that any public performance of music, including karaoke, is properly licensed and that all necessary permissions are obtained from the relevant performing rights organizations (PROs)," and the app itself "does not provide any licenses or permissions for public performance." — [My Karaoke Party Terms of Service](https://www.mykaraoke.party/terms-of-service)
- BallparkDJ's dedicated licensing page states directly: "There is a difference between having access to a song and having the rights needed to play that music publicly," and frames the responsibility as resting with "the park, league, school, municipality or facility," advising teams/parents to check with the facility rather than assume the app resolves this. — [BallparkDJ: Music Licensing](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)
- For venues using commercial karaoke platforms, "while karaoke platforms like KaraFun cover the content licensing, venues are still required to obtain a public performance license from their country's music rights organization" — i.e., even a platform that *does* license its own catalog still pushes the venue-level PRO obligation onto the customer. — [KaraFun Business help center, per search summary](https://business.karafun.com/helpcenter/278/)
- A second walk-up-song app (WalkUp, integrating Apple MusicKit and the Spotify iOS SDK) was checked directly and its marketing site contains **no** licensing disclaimer, ToS excerpt, or mention of PROs/performance rights at all — it is silent on the issue rather than explicit. — [WalkUp app site](https://walkup.framer.website/)
- A third app listing ("Baseball Walk-Up Songs," on the App Store) reportedly notes "Music must be owned or downloaded on your device," with terms linked to Apple's standard EULA rather than any bespoke licensing language. — summarized from App Store search result (not independently fetched; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- There appear to be two distinct postures among adjacent apps: (1) explicit disclaimer + responsibility-shifting language, modeled closely on karaoke-app ToS (My Karaoke Party, BallparkDJ), and (2) complete silence on the topic (WalkUp), which is arguably riskier for the *app vendor* since it neither limits its own exposure nor informs users, but also doesn't make any representation that could be relied upon by a user as an assurance of legality.
- No adjacent app was found claiming to *provide* a performance license to end users as part of a subscription (other than BallparkDJ's Epidemic-Sound-backed "fully licensed" tier, which is really the royalty-free workaround — see next section — rather than a PRO sublicense).

### Gaps
- Did not independently fetch/verify the "Baseball Walk-Up Songs" App Store listing's terms text or the "Walkout Song DJ" / "Next Batter Up" apps' terms — only have search-snippet-level information for those, not confirmed primary-source quotes. Should be treated as lower-confidence.
- Fitness-class music apps (e.g., Peloton-style instructor-cued playlists) and wedding/DJ-cueing apps (e.g., DJ software ToS) were targeted by search but did not return distinct, citable ToS language beyond the generic "Spotify/commercial use" material already captured below — this is a gap; the key questions about these specific categories' own ToS text were not independently confirmed.

## Does deep-linking into a user's own Spotify/Apple Music subscription (vs. storing/playing local MP3s) shift licensing responsibility onto the user/streaming service's terms?

### Takeaway
Both Spotify's and Apple Music's own consumer terms explicitly restrict use to personal/non-commercial settings and explicitly name businesses/public venues as excluded — Spotify's official support article says this directly, including a specific list of example venues ("bars, restaurants, schools, stores, salons, dance studios, radio stations, etc.") and redirects commercial users to a different, licensed product ("Soundtrack"). This means that deep-linking into a user's personal streaming subscription does not resolve the public-performance-licensing question — it just moves the ToS violation (and the underlying PRO licensing gap) from "stored MP3 with no license at all" to "personal-use-only subscription being used outside its licensed scope," which is arguably a distinct but still real risk, not a shift of responsibility that actually clears it.

### Cited Findings
- Spotify's own support article states: "Spotify is only for personal, non-commercial use," and explicitly prohibits broadcasting or playing Spotify publicly from businesses including "bars, restaurants, schools, stores, salons, dance studios, radio stations, etc." — [Spotify Support: Spotify for public or commercial use](https://support.spotify.com/us/article/spotify-public-commercial-use/)
- For commercial/public environments, Spotify's own guidance directs users elsewhere: "check out our friends at Soundtrack" (a separate, licensed-for-business music service) rather than claiming its own consumer product covers that use case. — [Spotify Support: Spotify for public or commercial use](https://support.spotify.com/us/article/spotify-public-commercial-use/)
- "Apple's terms specify that you may use the Services and Content only for personal, noncommercial purposes... Subscribers can use Apple Music only for personal, noncommercial purposes. Playing music from your personal Apple Music account in a commercial setting (e.g., store, restaurant, office) violates these terms, exposing your business to legal risks." Users additionally "agree not to modify, rent, loan, sell, share, or distribute the Services or Content in any manner" and not to "exploit the Services in any manner not expressly authorized." — [search summary of Apple Media Services Terms and Conditions](https://www.apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/)
- BallparkDJ's own licensing page draws exactly this distinction for the walk-up-song use case: "There is a difference between having access to a song and having the rights needed to play that music publicly" — explicitly flagging that an Apple Music subscription (access) is not the same thing as venue-level public performance rights. — [BallparkDJ: Music Licensing](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)

### Inferences
- A field/park is not literally a "bar, restaurant, school, store, salon, dance studio, or radio station," but it plainly falls within the same category Spotify is targeting (a public-facing, non-private setting with an audience outside the user's "circle of family and friends"), so an app that deep-links into a user's personal Spotify/Apple Music account to play a walk-up song over a field PA is very likely using that subscription outside its licensed personal/non-commercial scope — even though the *audio itself* is "licensed" in the sense that Spotify/Apple paid for streaming rights, those platform-level licenses are structured as personal-use streaming rights, not public-performance rights, and don't flow through to the end user's own public performance.
- From a product-liability-exposure standpoint, deep-linking (vs. storing MP3s locally) does shift *where* the audio file lives and *who controls playback*, which could matter for a DMCA/technical-infringement analysis (the app never touches copyrighted audio bytes), but it does not appear to change the underlying public-performance-licensing gap at the field — that gap is about the *performance* at the venue, not which app or service physically renders the audio.
- No source was found suggesting either Spotify or Apple Music's ToS carves out "public performance of a short clip" (e.g., a walk-up clip of 5-10 seconds) as distinct from playing a full track — the "personal non-commercial use" restriction as quoted makes no durational exception.

### Gaps
- Did not find a court case, regulatory guidance, or legal-commentary piece specifically analyzing whether "clip length" (e.g., a 10-second walk-up snippet vs. a full song) changes the public-performance-licensing analysis — this is a real open legal question, not addressed by any source found, and should be treated as unresolved rather than assumed favorable.
- Did not find Spotify's or Apple's raw terms-of-service legal text verbatim (only summarized/quoted via secondary sources and a WebFetch summary) — for a definitive quote, the primary ToS documents (open.spotify.com/legal, apple.com/legal/internet-services/itunes/) should be read directly before relying on exact wording in any user-facing copy.

## Do youth-sports or walk-up-song-adjacent apps use a royalty-free/licensed sound-clip library instead, to avoid touching copyrighted commercial music?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is a documented, real pattern: BallparkDJ, a walk-up-song app squarely in this product category, offers a "fully licensed" music tier built on Epidemic Sound (a pre-cleared, royalty-free music library) specifically so that tier carries no additional public-performance-licensing burden, while treating its other two tiers (commercial streaming-service music, and user-owned content) as carrying the licensing question through to the user/venue.

### Cited Findings
- BallparkDJ offers three music approaches: "Fully licensed music" through integrated Epidemic Sound (rights already covered); "Commercial music sources" like Apple Music (requires venue-level licensing verification, i.e., not resolved by the app); and "User-owned content" (original recordings, school band music). — [BallparkDJ: Music Licensing](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)
- Separately, general-purpose royalty-free "hype"/entrance/sports-themed audio libraries exist and are explicitly marketed for sports walkout/entrance use (e.g., Uppbeat's "Entrance" category is described as suited for "sports walkouts or dramatic reveals," and sites like Envato Elements and Pixabay offer "sports hype" and stadium-themed royalty-free tracks/SFX) — these are general-purpose libraries rather than apps built specifically for youth sports, but they represent the same underlying workaround (pre-cleared music instead of commercial hits). — [Uppbeat: Free Entrance Music](https://uppbeat.io/music/category/entrance); [Envato Elements: Royalty-Free Sports Hype Audio](https://elements.envato.com/audio/sports+hype); [Free Safe Music: Royalty Free Hype Music](https://freesafemusic.com/genres/hype/)

### Inferences
- Epidemic Sound (and similar subscription royalty-free libraries) is the specific mechanism other builders in this exact niche have reached for to sidestep PRO licensing entirely — which validates "ship a licensed sound-clip library as the default/safe path, and treat user-selected commercial songs as an explicit, disclosed, user-responsibility opt-in" as a workable, precedented product pattern rather than a novel idea.
- No evidence was found of a youth-sports app marketing a royalty-free library as its *only* option (i.e., refusing to let users pick real commercial songs at all) — the pattern found is "offer both, make the licensed option the default/safe one, and disclaim the other."

### Gaps
- Did not find usage/adoption data (e.g., what fraction of BallparkDJ users choose the Epidemic Sound tier vs. Apple Music) — can't speak to how commercially viable the royalty-free-only approach is versus one that still offers real commercial songs.
- Did not independently verify Epidemic Sound's own license terms as to whether they explicitly cover "live public performance at a venue" (as opposed to just covering sync use in video content, which is Epidemic Sound's more traditional core use case) — this matters because a royalty-free *video/sync* license is not automatically the same thing as a public-performance clearance for playing audio live over a PA system, and BallparkDJ's page did not spell out that distinction when I fetched it.
