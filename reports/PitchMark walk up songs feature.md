# Why Walk-Up Songs Should Stay Local

Walk-up songs are a solved UX problem wrapped in an unsolved licensing problem, and the dozen-plus indie apps already serving this market all made the same bet: store a short clip locally, play it with one tap through a Bluetooth speaker, and let the commercial-music question be the user's responsibility rather than the app's. At every amateur level — Little League, travel ball, high school, adult rec — the actual setup is a volunteer ("Team Mom," "team music parent") running a phone or tablet into a battery-powered Bluetooth speaker, not a stadium PA system ([WalkUpPro](https://walkuppro.com/blog/how-to-play-walkup-songs/)). The competitive field is crowded — at least 20 distinct named apps (BallparkDJ, Walkout Song DJ, WalkUp Songs, Now Batting, and others) converge on the identical mechanic of a pre-trimmed 10–20 second clip triggered by a single tap — yet none of the major platforms coaches already use (GameChanger, TeamSnap, SportsEngine) have built this in, and iScore's own developer explicitly deprioritized it as a small-audience request. Public-performance licensing (ASCAP/BMI/SESAC) is a genuine legal obligation that attaches to the venue, not the app, and every walk-up app examined either disclaims it explicitly or stays silent rather than resolving it. Technically, a locally-stored audio clip played through `AVAudioPlayer`/`AVAudioEngine` is the only one of three playback approaches immune to field connectivity problems and the only one with no streaming-service account dependency — Apple Music's MusicKit requires a subscription on the playing device, and Spotify's APIs are gated to 5 allowlisted users unless PitchMark reaches 250,000 monthly active users. For a first version, PitchMark's best move is to copy the converged pattern the market already validated rather than innovate past it.

## The team DJ, not the coach, owns this today

Across every level examined, walk-up music is run by a designated volunteer who is explicitly *not* the head coach — community sources call this role "Team Mom," "team music parent," or "a cohort who mans the iPod," and it exists as a distinct job separate from scorekeeping or PA announcing ([HitterHype](https://hitterhype.com/how-to-be-the-team-music-parent), [HSBaseballWeb](https://community.hsbaseballweb.com/topic/pa-announcers-responsibilities)). The equipment gradient tracks level fairly cleanly: Little League and most travel-ball tournament fields have no field PA at all and rely on a parent-carried Bluetooth speaker positioned against the backstop ([Umpire-Empire](https://umpire-empire.com/topic/75548-walk-up-music/)); high-school varsity fields are the one tier with a named, booth-operated PA role responsible for walk-up songs as part of standard duties ([HSBaseballWeb](https://community.hsbaseballweb.com/topic/pa-announcers-responsibilities)); and even where a booster-funded installed PA system exists, the described operating pattern reduces to "plug your phone into the aux input" — the PA is just an amplifier, not a managed system with its own interface ([vendor synthesis](https://walkamp.com/)). This matters for product design: there is no level of youth/amateur ball where a trained AV operator runs this, so the UI has to work for a non-technical volunteer under time pressure, and the app does not need to integrate with PA hardware directly — aux/Bluetooth-out from a phone is the universal interface regardless of venue tier.

The operational constraints that recur across vendor and independent sources alike are narrow and specific. Clips need to be pre-trimmed to roughly **10–20 seconds**, starting at "the most distinctive moment — the chorus or hook — rather than at the track's technical starting point" ([OnDeckDJ](https://www.ondeckdj.app/blog/how-to-play-walk-up-songs.html)), because the walk-up window itself is only that long and starting music too early "causes the 10-20 second window to expire prematurely" (same source). Switching between consecutive batters has to be a single tap — the documented failure mode of the manual approach it replaces is a volunteer "manually scroll[ing] to find each song during at-bats" while "songs start at the wrong moment and volume levels fluctuate unpredictably between tracks" (same source). Connectivity complaints that surfaced are about **Bluetooth pairing drops and app backend/internet-check errors**, not confirmed cellular dead zones specifically, but the advice that follows from them — keep a wired aux cable as backup, accept that skipping one at-bat is fine — points the same direction as a locally-cached-clip design regardless of which failure mode actually dominates at a given field ([OnDeckDJ](https://www.ondeckdj.app/blog/how-to-play-walk-up-songs.html)). Fade-out-on-pitch is aspirational "professional standard" language borrowed informally by youth commentators, not a codified rule anywhere in amateur baseball; an umpire-forum participant could only offer "there might be a general problem" if music ran into live play, not a settled rule ([Umpire-Empire](https://umpire-empire.com/topic/75548-walk-up-music/)), and a Texas NFHS-adjacent rules interpreter confirmed there is "no specific NFHS rule" and the operative standard is simply whether a sound *distracts or startles* ([DiscussFastpitch](https://www.discussfastpitch.com/threads/when-to-play-music-between-innings.37399/page-12)). A feature should give the operator a prominent manual stop, not assume any automatic, rule-driven cutoff point tied to pitch state.

Song intake is informal and under-documented. The two patterns that actually surface in sources are a centralized model (one volunteer collects everyone's pick by text or hallway conversation and enters it into one app or device — the pattern behind the one concrete anecdote found, a coach-pitch "Team Mom" who unilaterally downloaded BallparkDJ for her whole team) and a distributed model that newer dedicated apps are explicitly built to support, where "parents [are] able to add their player's walkout music while coaches stay in control of the lineup" ([Lineup DJ, via search synthesis](https://lineupdj.app/); [Country Music Dads](https://countrymusicdads.substack.com/p/play-ball-yall-country-walk-up-songs)). Notably, none of the plausible intake channels this task's own brief suggested — a shared spreadsheet, a TeamSnap announcement, a GroupMe thread — could be confirmed as actual documented practice; they are reasonable guesses, not verified behavior, and should be treated as unconfirmed.

## A crowded App Store niche no platform wants to touch

At least 20 distinct, independently verifiable products target this exact use case, led by **BallparkDJ**, which self-reports 177,000+ active accounts and 8.5 million+ players added across its history ([ballparkdj.com](https://www.ballparkdj.com/)), and **Walkout Song DJ**, which has run "since 2013" and carries 4.8★ across 12,000+ App Store ratings — an unusually large review count for a niche category ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/walkout-song-dj/id741990930)). The mechanism is essentially identical across the entire field: pick a short clip from a streaming catalog or local file, assign it to a roster slot, tap play at the right moment, route to a Bluetooth speaker. Not one of these apps does true automated, hands-free cueing tied to a live scoring event — a human taps play every time, which matches exactly what the operational research found teams actually want and tolerate.

The differentiation that does exist clusters around three features: AI-generated announcer voices (Now Batting offers six voice options plus auto-generated "original songs" using a player's name; Walk-Up Hype offers AI PA announcements with a coach-approval queue so parents self-submit songs via a shareable web link with no app install required — [nowbattingapp.com](https://www.nowbattingapp.com/), [walkuphype.com](https://walkuphype.com/)); optical/manual roster import from GameChanger or TeamSnap via QR code or photo of a lineup card, rather than any real API integration (WalkUpJams, Walkup Pro — [walkupjam.com](https://walkupjam.com/)); and the choice of music source, where Apple Music dominates and only one product ("Walk-Up," App Store id6743056436) integrates Spotify at all. Pricing across the category is cheap and mostly freemium: free tiers with 1-3 players, lifetime unlocks around $5–7, or annual plans in the $5–25 range ([Walkout Song DJ](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/walkout-song-dj/id741990930), [nowbattingapp.com](https://www.nowbattingapp.com/)). Tellingly, the major platforms coaches already use for everything else have stayed out: GameChanger's own marketing makes no mention of walk-up songs and focuses on live scoring and streaming ([GameChanger](https://gc.com/)); TeamSnap, SportsEngine, LeagueApps, and Hudl show no evidence of a native feature; and iScore Baseball's own forum shows the feature was requested by users and explicitly placed on a wish-list but **not prioritized**, because "only a small user base would potentially use it" (per a search-summary of the iscoreforum.com thread, not independently re-verified — [iscoreforum.com](http://iscoreforum.com/baseball/viewtopic.php?t=7750)). That combination — real, sustained grassroots demand (a dozen-plus apps, one with 12K+ ratings) alongside deliberate platform indifference — is the clearest signal in this research: walk-up songs are a genuine, recurring want at the team level, but the big platforms view it as a novelty beneath their core scorekeeping/communication value proposition, leaving the space to small, low-differentiation, solo-developer apps. For PitchMark, that is an opening rather than evidence the feature isn't worth building — it just means expectations should be set by what these small apps already do well, not by assuming a bar no one else has cleared.

Searches for entrance-music cueing in adjacent live-event sports (wrestling, boxing/MMA, roller derby, darts) turned up no dedicated venue-side cueing product at all — what exists are fan-facing soundboard/meme sites, not operator tools — which suggests baseball/softball's walk-up-song app micro-industry is unusual even within sports, likely because the batter-by-batter, many-times-per-game cueing need is distinctive to baseball's at-bat structure.

## Licensing risk is real, diffuse, and routinely pushed onto the end user

US copyright law requires a public performance license from the relevant performing-rights organization (PRO — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, or GMR) to play copyrighted recorded music anywhere outside a private family/friends setting, and the four PROs' catalogs don't overlap, so full legal coverage in practice means holding more than one license ([BMI FAQs](https://www.bmi.com/licensing/faqs)). High school athletics is the one tier with a clean, documented answer: the **NFHS has a blanket ASCAP license**, and BMI separately offers a "License for Primary and Secondary Schools" that explicitly lists sporting events and games as covered uses ([NFHS](https://nfhs.org/stories/copyright-compliance-with-music-and-sporting-events); [BMI school license](https://copyright.nfhs.org/bmi-license/)). ASCAP also maintains a named "Sports and Competitive Events" license whose own marketing copy explicitly lists "Amateur Leagues" among the entities it targets ([ASCAP](https://www.ascap.com/music-users/types/sports-and-competitive-events)) — but **no equivalent documented program surfaced for Little League, travel ball, or municipal rec leagues**; coverage there is inconsistent and ad hoc, dependent on whether a given parks department or private complex operator happens to hold a PRO license for unrelated reasons. The gap between "ASCAP sells a license amateur leagues could buy" and "a given Little League field actually has one" is the real risk surface, and it is unresolved in the available evidence either way.

Every existing walk-up-song app that addresses the issue at all pushes the obligation downstream rather than resolving it. BallparkDJ's own licensing page states plainly: "There is a difference between having access to a song and having the rights needed to play that music publicly," and assigns responsibility to "the park, league, school, municipality or facility — not the individual parent, coach or Team DJ pressing play," advising users to check with the facility operator rather than assume coverage ([BallparkDJ](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)). This mirrors how karaoke apps handle the identical problem: My Karaoke Party's terms restrict use to private, non-commercial settings and state the app "does not provide any licenses or permissions for public performance" ([My Karaoke Party ToS](https://www.mykaraoke.party/terms-of-service)). Other apps in the category are simply silent on the topic rather than explicit — a riskier posture for the vendor, since it neither limits exposure nor informs users, though it also makes no representation a user could rely on.

Deep-linking into a user's personal Apple Music or Spotify subscription does not close this gap; it relocates it. Both services' consumer terms restrict use to personal, non-commercial settings and name public venues explicitly: Spotify's own support page prohibits "broadcasting or playing Spotify publicly" at businesses including "bars, restaurants, schools, stores, salons, dance studios, radio stations, etc." and redirects commercial users to a separately licensed product, Soundtrack ([Spotify Support](https://support.spotify.com/us/article/spotify-public-commercial-use/)); Apple's Media Services terms similarly restrict subscription use to personal, noncommercial purposes. So an app that deep-links into a coach's personal streaming account to play a song over a dugout speaker is likely using that subscription outside its licensed scope, even though the underlying track itself is "licensed" in the sense that Spotify/Apple paid for streaming rights — those are personal-streaming rights, not public-performance rights, and nothing in either company's terms carves out a durational exception for a short clip. The one validated workaround in this market is a pre-cleared royalty-free library: BallparkDJ offers a "fully licensed" tier built on Epidemic Sound specifically so that tier carries no additional performance-licensing burden, while still offering commercial streaming music and user-owned content as separate, user-responsibility tiers ([BallparkDJ](https://www.ballparkdj.com/music-licensing)). No app was found that offers *only* a royalty-free library and refuses real commercial songs — the market pattern is "offer both, default to the safe option, disclose the rest," which is a workable, precedented posture rather than a novel risk PitchMark would be taking on alone.

## Local files are the only option immune to the field's constraints

Three playback architectures are viable, and they trade off almost exactly along the reliability-versus-capability axis the brief asks about. **Local audio clips** played through `AVAudioPlayer` or `AVAudioEngine` require no network connectivity at the moment of playback, which makes this the only approach structurally immune to a dead connection at a remote field — the tradeoff is that someone has to supply the clip (a recording or an upload), and Apple's App Store review guideline 1.2 for user-generated content (filtering, reporting, blocking, published contact info) plausibly attaches to any feature letting users upload audio, even in a closed-roster context. A 10–20 second AAC clip at a voice/music-adequate bitrate runs roughly 150–300 KB, so even a full 20–30-player roster is a trivial sync load over Firebase Cloud Storage, whose pricing ($0.026/GB-month storage, $0.12/GB egress after free tiers) is immaterial at this scale ([Firebase Pricing](https://firebase.google.com/pricing)). The real engineering risk here is not storage or playback APIs but audio-session routing: reliable Bluetooth A2DP/AirPlay output requires the `.playback` session category plus explicit `allowBluetoothA2DP`/`allowAirPlay` options, and there is a currently open iOS 26.5 compatibility bug where exactly that combination can cause session activation to fail ([GitHub issue #1115](https://github.com/software-mansion/react-native-audio-api/issues/1115)) — this needs hands-on verification against the current SDK before shipping, consistent with PitchMark's existing practice of verifying anything audio/video-related on-device rather than assuming documentation holds.

**Apple Music via MusicKit** is technically capable — a SwiftUI app can start a specific catalog track and seek to a timestamp — but comes with real strings attached: full-track playback requires an **active Apple Music subscription signed into that specific device** (non-subscribers get only a 30-second preview), Apple's App Store Guideline 4.5.2 requires the user to initiate playback and see standard transport controls (ruling out a fully silent one-tap-and-forget trigger with no player chrome), and monetizing access to the stream is explicitly prohibited ([App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Because subscription status is evaluated per signed-in account, the Pitchmark Display device — likely signed into its own Apple ID — would not automatically inherit the coach's subscription, a real architectural wrinkle for a two-device product. **Spotify** is the least viable path for PitchMark specifically: both its App Remote SDK and Web API require Spotify Premium, and production use beyond 5 allowlisted testers requires Extended Quota Mode, which as of a May 2025 policy change is restricted to registered businesses with **250,000+ monthly active users** — a bar no youth/amateur team-scale app could plausibly clear, capping any Spotify-based implementation at a handful of testers indefinitely ([Spotify Quota Modes docs](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-api/concepts/quota-modes)). This is also exactly why the competitive field skews so heavily toward Apple Music and local files: Apple's on-device MusicKit framework lets an app play catalog tracks from a signed-in subscription with no server-side OAuth dance, while Spotify's control plane requires Premium, OAuth, and now a scale threshold most of this market will never reach.

| Approach | Offline-capable at play time | Account/subscription needed | Review/guideline friction | Clip-start precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Local clip (upload/record) | Yes | None | UGC moderation obligations (1.2) | Full manual control |
| Apple Music / MusicKit | No (streams; preview-only without subscription) | Apple Music subscription, per device | Guideline 4.5.2: user-initiated, standard transport controls, no monetizing access | `startTime` has a documented, unresolved bug in `ApplicationMusicPlayer` |
| Spotify SDK/Web API | No | Spotify Premium; Extended Quota needs 250K+ MAU | No Spotify-specific guideline, falls under general 5.2.2 | `position_ms` works, but quota ceiling blocks real deployment |

Deep-linking out to the Apple Music or Spotify app (rather than embedding playback control) would likely draw less App Review scrutiny, since the user leaves PitchMark entirely and plays the track under that service's own terms — but this abandons the "stay in PitchMark, hear it over the dugout speaker" product goal that is the entire point of the feature, so it is not a serious contender for a first version.

## Conclusion

The research converges on an unusually clean answer for a product question: the market has already run this experiment at scale, across more than 20 independent apps and over a decade (Walkout Song DJ since 2013), and it settled on one mechanism — a short, pre-trimmed local-feeling clip, one-tap-triggered, Bluetooth-out — not because no one tried streaming integrations, but because streaming integrations run into subscription requirements, account-tied limitations, and (for Spotify) a scale wall that makes them impractical for this exact audience. PitchMark doesn't need to discover the right architecture; it needs to adopt the one the category already converged on, and lean on infrastructure (Firebase Storage sync, AVFoundation audio-session handling) it most likely already has in some form from its Moments video/audio work.

The one place PitchMark should differ from the existing field is licensing posture, precisely because every competitor's current approach is to disclaim responsibility rather than help the user discharge it. Given that NFHS-affiliated high schools already have documented blanket coverage and youth/travel-ball venues are the genuinely uncovered tier, a short, clear in-app disclosure at the point a coach picks a song — modeled on BallparkDJ's own framing that "access" and "rights" are different things — is cheap, precedented, and the only piece of this feature where simply copying the incumbents would mean copying their most legally exposed habit (silence).

## Recommended MVP scope

- **Local clips only for v1** — let a coach or parent record or upload a short (10–20 second) audio clip per player, stored via Firebase Storage and attached to the existing Player model; play it with `AVAudioPlayer`/`AVAudioEngine` through the device's Bluetooth/AirPlay output. No Apple Music or Spotify integration in the first release — both add subscription dependencies, review friction, or (for Spotify) a scale cap PitchMark cannot clear.
- **One-tap play with a prominent manual stop**, not any automatic fade tied to pitch state — there's no sensor or rule to trigger an automatic cutoff, and the existing apps all rely on a human tapping play/stop anyway.
- **Simple, informal song intake** — let whoever already manages the roster (coach, or a designated parent) attach/replace a clip per player in the existing Player/Team UI; don't build a dedicated submission workflow, approval queue, or external-link intake for v1 — none of that is confirmed as real practice even among competitors, several of whom are still iterating on it.
- **A brief, visible licensing disclosure at the point of picking a song** (e.g., "you're responsible for checking whether this venue/league has music performance rights covered"), rather than silence — this is the one place to do better than the incumbents, at near-zero engineering cost.
- **Defer Display-device playback and cross-device sync** of the song itself to a later version; treat v1 as coach-device-only, since the companion Display app signed into its own account would complicate any streaming-subscription approach and isn't needed for a locally-stored-clip MVP anyway.
- **Verify Bluetooth/AirPlay output on-device early**, given the open iOS compatibility issue combining `.playback` category with `allowBluetoothA2DP`/`allowAirPlay` — this is the one technical risk in the local-clip path that isn't just documentation-level, and per PitchMark's own verification constraints, touch/audio behavior has to be confirmed on a real device, not assumed from docs.
